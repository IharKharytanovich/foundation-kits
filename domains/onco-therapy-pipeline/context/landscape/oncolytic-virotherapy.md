# Oncolytic Virotherapy

[[oncolytic-virotherapy]] uses a virus engineered to replicate selectively in tumor cells and lyse them. The resulting immunogenic cell death releases tumor antigens and danger signals in situ, turning a "cold" tumor "hot" and priming a systemic anti-tumor T-cell response that can reach non-injected (abscopal) lesions.

**Maturity: approved (T-VEC) to clinical (RP1 and pipeline). Personalization type: off-the-shelf**, delivered **locally / intratumorally**.

## T-VEC Baseline and RP1 / IGNYTE

**Talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC)**, an HSV-1 vector expressing GM-CSF, was approved in 2015 and remains the **only FDA-approved oncolytic virus** (unresectable melanoma). The next-generation **RP1 (vusolimogene oderparepvec)** plus nivolumab reported **confirmed ORR 32.9%** with ~15% complete responses in anti-PD-1-failed melanoma (IGNYTE), but the FDA issued a Complete Response Letter — **RP1 is not yet approved** (see `sources/rp1-ignyte-2025.md`).

## Delivery by Injection, Not Biodistribution

Oncolytic viruses are administered **intratumorally**, placing the payload directly inside the lesion. This bypasses the systemic solid-tumor delivery wall — the modality never has to solve IV biodistribution or endosomal escape into distant tumor cells, because the clinician injects the target directly. The systemic benefit then rides on the immune response the local lysis provokes, not on the virus reaching distant sites.

## Adjacency to Neoantigen Vaccination

The therapeutic mechanism is the patient's own tumor antigens being presented after lysis — an in-situ analogue of an individualized vaccine. Where an [[neoantigen-vaccine]] selects and manufactures defined neoepitopes ex vivo, oncolytic lysis liberates the tumor's full antigen repertoire in place; both aim to convert tumor antigens into a T-cell response.
