# Engineered Tumor-Targeting Bacteria

[[bacterial-tumor-delivery]] is the one platform that exploits, rather than fights, the [[tumor-hypoxia]] that defeats every other delivery method. Obligate and facultative anaerobes germinate and proliferate exactly in the hypoxic-necrotic tumor core that blood-borne drugs cannot reach and that is radioresistant — a genuine bypass of the [[solid-tumor-delivery]] wall, with the added advantage that the payload self-amplifies as the bacteria replicate.

## The Hypoxia-Seeking Rationale

The tumor core is inaccessible to drugs and hostile to immune cells, but ideal for anaerobic bacteria, which grow there while being cleared from oxygenated normal tissue — producing, preclinically, tumor-to-normal accumulation ratios above 1000:1 for Salmonella.

## The Clinical Reality — Strong Selectivity, Modest Delivery

- **Clostridium novyi-NT** (obligate anaerobe, intratumoral): in a phase 1 of 24 refractory-tumor patients, spores germinated in 42% (tumor-confined lysis) and produced injected-tumor shrinkage in 41%, with 86% stable disease — but with serious infection toxicity (grade 4 sepsis, gas gangrene) gated by tumor size.
- **Salmonella VNP20009** (facultative anaerobe, intravenous): safely dosed in 25 melanoma patients but colonized only 3/25 tumors with no objective responses — the >1000:1 preclinical selectivity did not translate to reliable human systemic colonization, because attenuation for safety reduced colonization efficiency and IV dosing hits the cytokine-toxicity ceiling first.

## The Living Drug Factory (Chowdhury 2019)

The most compelling mechanistic case: a non-pathogenic E. coli engineered with a synchronized lysis circuit periodically self-lyses to release an anti-CD47 nanobody in the tumor. In mice it drove complete regression (~80% survival past 90 days), an abscopal effect on untreated tumors, and durable immune memory, while staying confined to the injected lesion. Bacteria act as self-amplifying, tumor-restricted immune-agonist factories.

## The Trade-off

Bacteria uniquely solve tropism, hypoxia penetration, and self-amplification — but carry infection/toxicity risk, and systemic (IV) colonization remains unreliable in humans. The most reliable clinical use is intratumoral, and the frontier (E. coli Nissle synthetic circuits) is early-clinical without peer-reviewed human colonization data yet.
