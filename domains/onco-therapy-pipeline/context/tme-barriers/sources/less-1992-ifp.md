# Less/Boucher/Jain 1992 — Human Tumor Interstitial Fluid Pressure

The canonical human interstitial-fluid-pressure (IFP) dataset, still the headline anchor for the pressure barrier.

## Design & Methods

Primary clinical measurement study using the wick-in-needle technique to measure interstitial fluid pressure directly in human tumors and adjacent normal tissue.

## Quantitative Results

- **Tumors (41 measurements across 21 tumors): IFP 4–50 mmHg, mean 20 ± 13 mmHg.**
- **Normal tissues (11 measurements): mean 2 ± 4 mmHg** (near zero).
- By tumor type: metastatic melanoma **33 ± 14**; colorectal liver metastases **21 ± 12**; primary breast carcinoma **15 ± 9**; recurrent renal cell **38** (n=1) mmHg.
- Prior human series (cited): superficial melanoma 14.3 ± 12.5 (n=12), cervical 15.7 ± 5.7 (n=12), head-and-neck 13.2 ± 8.8 (n=19) mmHg.

## Limitations

Small n; single-timepoint; surface-accessible tumors; older methodology — but reproduced by later series (e.g. non-invasive 4T1 breast model IFP 3–16 mmHg).

## Conclusion

Solid tumors are uniformly interstitially hypertensive. Because tumor IFP approaches normal microvascular pressure, the transvascular pressure gradient that normally drives convection into tissue collapses, producing outward convective flow at the tumor periphery that physically opposes the inward transport of drugs, nanoparticles, and cells — the mechanistic basis for the interstitial-fluid-pressure barrier and a primary reason the EPR effect fails in humans.
