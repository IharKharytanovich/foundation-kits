# Endosomal Escape — The Universal 1–2% Ceiling

Dalabehera et al. 2025 reviewed the mechanisms and quantitative efficiency of endosomal escape across lipid-based and polymeric nanoparticle delivery systems for nucleic acid therapeutics.

## Key Findings

**Endosomal escape efficiency: 1–2% across modalities.** Following receptor-mediated endocytosis, nanoparticles are trafficked through early endosomes to late endosomes and lysosomes. Only 1–2% of internalized cargo successfully escapes to the cytoplasm before lysosomal degradation. This figure is consistent across LNPs, polyplexes, and lipoplexes.

**Ionizable lipid mechanism.** For LNPs, endosomal escape depends on the ionizable lipid component becoming protonated at endosomal pH (~5.5–6.5). Protonation induces a conformational shift from cylindrical to inverted hexagonal (HII) phase, which disrupts the endosomal membrane. The optimal pKa range for ionizable lipids is 6.2–6.5 — lipids with pKa below 6.0 are insufficiently protonated at endosomal pH, while those above 6.8 are too protonated at physiological pH and show toxicity.

**Quantification methods.** The review consolidated data from galectin-8 recruitment assays (GFP-Gal8 puncta counting), split-GFP endosomal escape reporters, and electron microscopy membrane disruption imaging. All methods converge on the 1–2% estimate, though absolute values vary by assay sensitivity.

**Strategies to improve escape.** Approaches include fusogenic peptides (GALA, HA2), photochemical internalization (PCI), osmotic peptides, and helper lipids (DOPE, cholesterol analogs). While several strategies showed 2–5× improvement in vitro, none has broken through 10% escape efficiency in vivo, and many cause unacceptable membrane toxicity at effective concentrations.

**The compounding loss.** Combined with the 0.7% ID tumor accumulation from systemic injection, the effective cytoplasmic delivery of editor cargo to a solid tumor cell is on the order of 0.007–0.014% of the injected dose — roughly 1 in 10,000 administered molecules.

## Impact

This review establishes endosomal escape as a biophysical ceiling that is orthogonal to the biodistribution problem. Even if tumor accumulation were solved (e.g., via local injection), the endosomal escape bottleneck would still limit cytoplasmic delivery to the low single-digit percent range. The two barriers multiply, making systemic delivery to solid tumors extraordinarily inefficient.
